# Common English Errors — Types and Severity

> When to read: editing a draft by an EAL author; prioritising fixes. Source:
> Cargill & O'Connor (2009), Ch. 17.2.

Communicating meaning clearly is the crucial factor. Editors consistently say
the same thing:

> "As long as the science is good and can be clearly understood, I don't worry
> too much about the English — I have copy editors who can fix that."
> (Editor of an Australian-based international journal)

But "clearly understood" sets a real bar. The eight error types below are
those EAL authors make most often. Sort them by how seriously they interfere
with meaning.

## The eight error types

| # | Error | Example |
|---|-------|---------|
| 1 | Wrong singular/plural | "all tea leaves sample were oven dried" |
| 2 | Over-complex / inaccurate grammatical structures | "This may be due to lower pH hinders dissolution of soil organic matter…" |
| 3 | Subject–verb non-agreement | "the results of this study suggests that…" |
| 4 | Wrong preposition | "similar with the results of other researchers" |
| 5 | Non-standard article use (a/an/the) | "the accumulation of Cu in human body" |
| 6 | Non-standard modal verb choice | *would* vs *will*, *can* vs *could* vs *may* |
| 7 | Wrong part of speech | "drought resistance varieties" |
| 8 | Non-conventional tense | present tense to refer to the study's own results |

## Severity for meaning

Sort each error into one of three columns. The column determines priority.

| Rarely / slightly affects meaning | Sometimes / moderately | Often / seriously |
|-----------------------------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Wrong preposition (4) — usually the meaning survives | Article errors (5) — usually readable, occasionally ambiguous | Over-complex structures (2) — can break parse entirely |
| Part of speech (7) — often obvious from context | Singular/plural (1) — usually obvious | Subject–verb agreement (3) — confuses what the subject is |
| Modal verb choice (6) — affects nuance, not gist | Tense (8) — can blur when an event happened | |

Your priorities for **self-editing** should follow the right-hand column first.
An over-complex sentence that a reader cannot parse is far more damaging than
a missing article.

## Strategies by error type

- **Over-complex structures (2)** — write short sentences first (two clauses
  only), then join if needed.
- **Subject–verb agreement (3)** — when editing, find the grammatical subject
  (not the nearest noun) and check the verb agrees.
- **Tense (8)** — keep a tense table for each section (see
  `academic-writing` Quick Reference). Default Results = past, Discussion
  claims = present + modal.
- **Articles (5)** — use the generic/specific flowchart in
  `academic-writing/references/english-articles.md`. Rule of thumb: NP1 +
  *of* + NP2 → *the* ~85% of the time.
- **Modal verbs (6)** — match the modal to the claim strength ladder
  (`demonstrate` > `indicate` > `appears` > `suggests`; `will` > `may`).

## A self-editing technique (EAL authors)

1. After the **science** is settled, switch focus to grammar only.
2. Print the text. Use a ruler to expose one sentence at a time, starting from
   the **last** sentence of a section and moving upward.
3. For each sentence check: verb–subject agreement, singular/plural, tense,
   articles. Reading backwards stops content distraction.
4. Use the word processor's Find for your own recurring mistakes.
5. Keep the spell-checker on, but remember it will not catch *there/their*,
   *it's/its*, or correctly-spelt wrong words.

## What editors actually care about

From the Elsevier editors' forum:

> "For the researcher and for the reviewer, we should emphasise the scientific
> contents of their work. Language skills should not be the barrier."

> "The authors may have important data, which is useful for the community, and
> must be helped."

Implication: prioritise clarity over elegance. A short, plain sentence that
communicates the science beats an elegant sentence that obscures it.
