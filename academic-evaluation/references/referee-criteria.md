# Referee Criteria — Where to Look

> When to read: running a complete referee-style review. Source: Cargill &
> O'Connor (2009), Ch. 3 and 13.

The 13 questions below are typical of Referee's Evaluation Forms for science
journals. For each, the table shows where in the manuscript the referee
expects to find the evidence (A = Abstract, I = Introduction, M = Methods,
R = Results, D = Discussion, Ref = reference list).

| # | Criterion | Where |
|---|-----------|-------|
| 1 | Is the contribution new? | I (Stages 2–4), D |
| 2 | Is the contribution significant? | I (Stage 3, optional Stage 5), D (implications) |
| 3 | Is it suitable for this journal? | Whole paper vs. journal scope |
| 4 | Is the organization acceptable? | Whole paper — AIMRaD coherence |
| 5 | Do methods and treatment of results conform to acceptable scientific standards? | M, R |
| 6 | Are all conclusions firmly based in the data presented? | D vs. R |
| 7 | Is the length satisfactory? | Whole paper vs. journal limits |
| 8 | Are all illustrations required? | Figures/tables vs. story |
| 9 | Are all figures and tables necessary? | Figures/tables vs. text duplication |
| 10 | Are figure legends and table titles adequate? | Each legend/title |
| 11 | Do the title and Abstract clearly indicate the content? | Title, Abstract |
| 12 | Are references up to date, complete, and correctly abbreviated? | Ref + in-text |
| 13 | Is the paper excellent, good, or poor? | Overall |

## How to use this list

For each criterion, answer **Yes / No / Partial**, with a sentence of
evidence (a quote or a location). A "No" or "Partial" becomes a reviewer
comment to send back to the author (if you are pre-reviewing) or to fix (if
you are self-reviewing).

## The recommendation scale

Referees typically tick one of:

- Accept without alteration
- Accept after minor revision
- Review again after major revision
- Reject

When **you** are pre-reviewing, give the equivalent recommendation: which of
these four is the draft heading for if nothing changes?

## Titles as content signposts

A good title (criterion 11) does three jobs at once:

- Identifies the **field** of the research
- Indicates the **story** the results tell
- Raises **questions** that make the reader want to read on

Example (Cargill):
> Title: "Bird use of rice field strips of varying width in the Kanto Plain
> of central Japan"

A reader extracts:
- Focus: birds in relation to rice fields
- Variable: width of strips
- Location: central Japan

And anticipates questions: which widths matter most, for which birds, measured
how, generalisable elsewhere?

If a title does **not** let a reader extract this kind of information, flag
it under criterion 11.

## Rating the paper overall (criterion 13)

Beyond the binary criteria, an overall judgement weighs:

- Does the paper **add** something to the field? (novelty + significance)
- Is the evidence **convincing**? (methods + results + claim strength)
- Is the paper **readable** by the intended audience? (organisation +
  language)
- Does it **fit** the journal's scope and standard?

Be honest. A weak "good" that the editor rejects wastes everyone's time; a
harsh "poor" on solid work that needs restructuring undersells it.
