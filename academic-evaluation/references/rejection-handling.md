# Handling Rejection

> When to read: a manuscript has been rejected and you must decide what to do.
> Source: Cargill & O'Connor (2009), Ch. 14.2 (Table 14.1).

Every experienced researcher has rejection stories. Successful authors are
successful at **dealing with** rejection, not avoiding it. The reasons for
rejection determine the response.

## Reasons for rejection → response options

| Reason for rejection | Response option 1 | Response option 2 |
|----------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Out of scope** — too specialised, wrong subject area, or insufficient general interest for the journal's readership | The editor usually decided this before review. Revise the manuscript and submit to a more appropriate journal (check your ranked list). | If it **was** reviewed, use the referees' reports (apply Rules of thumb 2 and 3 from `response-strategies.md`) and submit to a more appropriate journal. |
| **Clear and obvious flaws in the science** | The editor may have decided before review. Revise using the response guide; try to publish the best parts. | — |
| **Poor language or structure** — could not be sent to referees | The editor may have decided before review. Revise using the response guide; resubmit or submit elsewhere. | — |
| **High-ranking journal rejecting despite mostly positive reviews** | Examine the editor's letter for any encouragement to resubmit ("revise and resubmit"). If present, revise and resubmit (Rules 2 and 3). | If resubmission isn't encouraged, revise using the referee reports and submit to the next journal on your list. |
| **Referees misread or misunderstood the paper; recommendations unclear or misinterpreted; referee annoyed or unhelpful** | You can appeal to the editor — unlikely to succeed unless a major error of judgement occurred. Always make the uncontroversial changes first, before appealing, resubmitting, or going elsewhere. | Revise and resubmit, or submit to a new journal. Clarify any issues that caused the problems. If resubmitting to the same journal, note in your letter any misunderstanding, supportive referee comments, and improvements. |

## Decision checklist after a rejection

1. **Read the editor's letter carefully** — decide whether it is a true
   rejection, a "revise and resubmit" dressed in polite language, or a
   conditional acceptance. If unsure, show the letter to a colleague.
2. **Identify the reason** from the table above.
3. **Decide between**:
   - Revise and resubmit to the same journal (only if encouraged).
   - Revise and submit to the next journal on your ranked list.
   - Appeal (rare; only for clear editor/referee errors of judgement).
   - Substantially rewrite or do additional work.
4. **Use the referee reports regardless.** Even a rejection contains useful
   feedback. Apply Rules of thumb 2 and 3 from `response-strategies.md`.
5. **Do not retaliate.** A hostile response letter burns the journal for years.
6. **Move quickly.** A fast, professional resubmission recovers most of the
   lost time.

## Conditional acceptance vs. revise-and-resubmit

The difference is usually only in the editor's wording. If in doubt, treat
"revise and resubmit" as if it were conditional acceptance and do the work.
If you have a conditional acceptance, **none** of the comments is enough to
stop you publishing — the task is to maintain the story's integrity while
accommodating the reviewers.

## Emotional dimension

Reviewers' comments can feel personal. They rarely are. Dealing with comments
is part of the publishing process. If a comment is unclear or it annoys you,
leave it for a few days (not more than a week) before responding. Discuss
difficult comments with co-authors. Then return with the Rules of thumb in
mind.
