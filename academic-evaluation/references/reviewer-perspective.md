# Reviewer and Editor Perspective — How the Process Works

> When to read: understanding what reviewers/editors do; the five practices of
> successful authors. Source: Cargill & O'Connor (2009), Ch. 13.

A research article does not produce truth — it documents observations,
analysis, and interpretation. Peer review tests whether new information is
worth knowing and acting on. Understanding the roles helps you pre-empt
objections.

## The five practices of successful authors

1. **Review manuscripts themselves** (for colleagues and journals) — builds a
   framework for critique.
2. **Plan research and writing** to meet the quality criteria referees will
   impose.
3. **Select the journal carefully** and prepare the manuscript to maximise
   acceptance.
4. **Use structured review and pre-review** from colleagues before submitting.
5. **Use referee reports to improve** the manuscript, and show the editor how
   improvements were made.

## What peer review contributes

- Confirms hypotheses were **tested appropriately** and results reflect the
  methods and analysis.
- Confirms the **strength of claims** is appropriate to the evidence.
- Assists the journal to decide on **focus, novelty, and importance**.
- Checks **presentation and style** conform to conventions.
 Advises authors and editor on **how** (and where) to improve.

## The editor's role

The editor maintains the journal's reputation. They:

- Read the manuscript and decide whether to send it to review (reject without
  review only if out of scope, poor language/structure, or clear scientific
  flaws).
- Choose referees — usually two or more.
- Read the referee reports and decide the response. If referees disagree, may
  seek a third opinion.
- Write the decision letter to the corresponding author.

A well-prepared manuscript reporting science appropriate to the journal is
unlikely to be rejected without review.

## The reviewer's role

Reviewers:

- Are usually expert in the **general** field (not necessarily the exact
  subject).
- Have almost always published in the field (their work may be cited in your
  paper).
- Are **busy** with their own work — limited time and patience.
- Have their **own preferences and biases**.
- Are unpaid; reviewing is a professional contribution.

The refereeing process is **blind** — you do not know who reviewed your paper.
Double-blind (referees also don't know the authors) is less common in the
sciences.

## The contributor's covering letter

The covering letter is your chance to **sell** the paper to the editor. Use it
to:

- State the title and authors.
- State the work is new and original.
- Express belief the paper is within the journal's scope.
- Highlight what is novel and significant.
- Flag anything that may raise questions (a long paper, necessary photographs).
- Say you look forward to the referees' comments.

Example (abridged, Cargill Fig. 13.1):

> Dear Dr Brown,
>
> Please find attached the manuscript "Arbuscular mycorrhizal associations of
> the southern Simpson Desert". … There have been few studies of the ecology
> of the plants in this desert and little is known about how mycorrhizal
> associations are distributed amongst the desert plants of Australia. We
> report the arbuscular mycorrhizal status of 47 plant species for the first
> time. The manuscript has been prepared according to the journal's
> Instructions for Authors. We believe that this new work is within the scope
> of your journal…
>
> We await your response and the comments of reviewers.

## Pre-review as rehearsal

The best way to understand review is to do it. Options:

- **Journal club** — 3–10 colleagues meet regularly; each writes a referee's
  report on a chosen article using the 13-criteria form, then the group
  discusses strengths and weaknesses.
- **Pre-review a colleague's draft** using the 22-point checklist
  (`pre-review-checklist.md`).
- **Ask a senior colleague to share** a past submission's referee reports and
  response letter; analyse what worked.

See `academic-writing/references/manuscript-process.md` for the integration
with the drafting cycle.
