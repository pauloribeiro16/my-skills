# Britton-Simmons & Abbott (2008) — Annotated PEA

> Provided Example Article 2 from Cargill & O'Connor (2009), Ch. 19.
> Citation: Britton-Simmons, K.H. & Abbott, K.C. (2008). Short- and long-term
> effects of disturbance and propagule pressure on a biological invasion.
> *Journal of Ecology* 96, 68–77.

This PEA is more accessible than Kaiser et al. for readers outside molecular
biology. It demonstrates combined experimental + modelling work, a numbered
Abstract format, and clear claim-strength calibration.

## Title — noun phrase, keywords front-loaded

> "Short- and long-term effects of disturbance and propagule pressure on a
> biological invasion"

- **Noun phrase** form (traditional).
- Keywords up front: *disturbance*, *propagule pressure*, *biological
  invasion*.
- Signals two time scales (short/long) — a distinctive contribution.

## Abstract — numbered *Journal of Ecology* format

The journal requires numbered statements with the final point headed
**Synthesis**:

> 1. Invading species typically need to overcome multiple limiting factors
>    simultaneously… *[B + P]*
> 2. We used the invasion of marine algal communities… *[M]*
> 3. Our experimental results demonstrate that… neither disturbance nor
>    propagule input alone was sufficient… *[R — strong claim]*
> 4. The model showed that both the size and spatial arrangement of herbivore
>    disturbances had a major impact… *[R]*
> 5. *Synthesis.* Both the short-term experiment and the long-term model show
>    that *S. muticum* invasion success is co-regulated… *[C]*

The Synthesis point is required to be accessible to non-specialists — a
deliberate device to maximise the paper's impact.

## Introduction — exemplary Stage 1 → Stage 4 flow

Stage 1 opens with the broad importance:
> "Biological invasions are a global problem with substantial economic
> (Pimentel et al. 2005) and ecological (Mack et al. 2000) costs."

Stage 2 narrows to prior work on two factors (propagule pressure; resource
availability/disturbance) — **paragraph by paragraph**, each opening with a
topic sentence:
> "Propagule pressure is widely recognized as an important factor that
> influences invasion success…"
> "Resource availability is a second key factor known to influence invasion
> success…"

Stage 3 — **signal words everywhere**:
> "However, understanding how these processes interact to regulate invasions
> **remains a major challenge** in ecology…"
> "Despite its acknowledged importance, propagule pressure has **rarely** been
> manipulated experimentally and the interaction… is **not well understood**."
> "It is **presently unclear** how different disturbance agents influence
> long-term patterns of invasion."

Stage 4 — aim stated with verbs matching the dual experimental + modelling
design:
> "In this study we used the invasion… as a study system to better understand
> the effects of propagule pressure and disturbance on invasion. In a
> factorial field experiment we manipulated both… We supplement the
> experimental results with a parameterized integrodifference equation
> model…"

## Methods — strategy 1 + 2 alignment

Subheadings in Methods (STUDY SYSTEM, THE INVADER, FIELD EXPERIMENT,
STATISTICAL ANALYSIS, MODEL) map onto the Results structure. Each Methods
paragraph opens linking back:

> "The disturbance treatment had two levels: control and disturbed. Control
> plots were…"

Active/passive mixing for flow (note how passive links old info forward):
> "We used the results of these analyses to inform the construction of
> mechanistic candidate functions… *These candidate functions* **were
> compared** using differences in the Akaike information criteria… We then
> used model averaging…"

## Results — figures carry the story

Figure 1 shows recruits and adults against propagule pressure for both
disturbance treatments. The legend is **story-telling**:

> "Number of *Sargassum muticum* (a) recruits and (b) adults in field
> experiment plots (900 cm²). Propagule pressure is grams of reproductive
> tissue suspended over experimental plots at beginning of experiment. The
> average mass of an adult *S. muticum* (174 g) is indicated by an arrow.
> Data are means ± 1 SE (*n* = 3)."

All five legend parts present: title, what is shown, additional context,
units/statistics, symbol explanation.

Prose highlights the key interaction — **no commentary**:
> "The field experiment showed that recruitment of *S. muticum* was higher in
> plots that were disturbed compared to control plots… a significant
> interaction between disturbance and propagule pressure (F₅,₂₄ = 3.77,
> P = 0.01) indicates that the plots in the two disturbance treatments
> differed in the extent to which they were limited by propagule
> availability."

## Discussion — claim strength scaled to evidence

Strong (present-tense main verb, present-tense that-clause):
> "Our experimental results **demonstrate** that space- and
> propagule-limitation both **regulate** S. muticum recruitment."

Strong (present + future prediction):
> "These results **indicate** that S. muticum recruitment under natural field
> conditions **will be determined** by the interaction between disturbance and
> propagule input."

The strength is earned by the experimental design (factorial, replicated,
model-averaged).

## What to study in this article

- **Topic sentences** in every Introduction paragraph — predict the paragraph
  from its first sentence.
- **Signal words** for the gap — *however*, *remains a major challenge*,
  *rarely*, *not well understood*, *presently unclear*.
- **Numbered Abstract** with Synthesis — the journal's mandated format.
- **Active/passive mixing** to keep old information at the front of the
  sentence.
- **Claim-strength calibration**: "demonstrate" where the experiment is
  decisive, "indicate … will be" for model extrapolation.
