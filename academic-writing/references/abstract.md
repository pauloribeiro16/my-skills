# Writing the Abstract (Summary)

> When to read: writing or revising the Abstract. Source: Cargill & O'Connor
> (2009), Ch. 11.

## Why the Abstract matters

- For busy readers, it may be the **only** part they read.
- For readers with limited literature access, it may be the **only** part
  available.
- Abstracting services use title + Abstract + keywords for their searchable
  databases.
- It is usually the **first** thing a referee sees.

Write it **last**. It is a distillation of the finished paper.

## Information elements (B/P/M/R/C)

A full Abstract contains five elements (Weissberg & Buker 1990):

| Code | Element |
|------|---------|
| **B** | Some **background** information |
| **P** | The principal **activity/purpose** of the study and its scope |
| **M** | Information about the **methods** used |
| **R** | The most important **results** |
| **C** | A statement of **conclusion** or recommendation |

Often compressed to three:

| Code | Element |
|------|---------|
| **P + M** | Purpose + method |
| **R** | Results |
| **C** | Conclusion (+ recommendations) |

## Word limits

Journals set a maximum (e.g., 250 for *The Plant Journal*; 350 for the
*Journal of Ecology*). Some require **numbered statements** (e.g., the
*Journal of Ecology* requires the final point to be headed **Synthesis** and
emphasise the key findings and general significance, accessible to
non-specialists).

Always check the target journal's Instructions to Contributors.

## Hard rules

- **No references** in the Abstract.
- **No figures, tables, or illustrations**.
- **No discussion or judgemental statements** ("Surprisingly, we observed…",
  "We believe…").
- Every method mentioned must have a corresponding result; every claim must be
  backed by data presented.

## Drafting process

1. Write one or two sentences for each of B, P, M, R, C — drawing directly
   from the finished sections.
2. Combine into a first draft.
3. Count words against the journal limit. Shorten using techniques observed in
   abstracts you've analysed.
4. Check that the conclusion is **directly related** to the objective and
   primary endpoint.

## Example (Britton-Simmons & Abbott 2008, *Journal of Ecology*)

The numbered format maps cleanly onto the elements:

> 1. *[B+P]* Invading species typically need to overcome multiple limiting
>    factors simultaneously in order to become established, and understanding
>    how such factors interact to regulate the invasion process remains a
>    major challenge in ecology.
> 2. *[M]* We used the invasion of marine algal communities by the seaweed
>    *Sargassum muticum* as a study system to experimentally investigate the
>    independent and interactive effects of disturbance and propagule
>    pressure in the short term. Based on our experimental results, we
>    parameterized an integrodifference equation model, which we used to
>    examine how disturbances created by different benthic herbivores
>    influence the longer term invasion success of *S. muticum*.
> 3. *[R]* Our experimental results demonstrate that in this system neither
>    disturbance nor propagule input alone was sufficient to maximize
>    invasion success. Rather, the interaction between these processes was
>    critical…
> 4. *[R]* The model showed that both the size and spatial arrangement of
>    herbivore disturbances had a major impact…
> 5. *[C — Synthesis]* Both the short-term experiment and the long-term model
>    show that *S. muticum* invasion success is co-regulated by disturbance
>    and propagule pressure. Our results underscore the importance of
>    considering interactive effects when making predictions about invasion
>    success.

## Additional keywords

Beyond the title's words, select keywords from the indexing service's list.
Predict what your audience will search for. See also `journal-selection.md`
for how keywords interact with discoverability.
