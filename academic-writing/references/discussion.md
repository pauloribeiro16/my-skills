# Writing the Discussion section

> When to read: drafting the Discussion. Source: Cargill & O'Connor (2009),
> Ch. 9.

## Structural decisions first

Before drafting, answer:

- Does the target journal allow a **combined** Results/Discussion followed by
  a separate Conclusion? Would that suit your story?
- Does the journal allow **subheadings** in the Discussion? They help signal
  key messages.
- Should the **title** be redrafted to reflect the elements you'll emphasise
  in the Discussion?

## Relate the Discussion to the Introduction

- The Discussion must connect to the issues raised in the Introduction — the
  "country" where you began, the evidence leading to your Stage 3 gap, and
  your Stage 4 purpose.
- When the first Discussion draft is ready, go back and check the
  Introduction for close fit. Redraft the Introduction if needed.
- Do **not** repeat all the Introduction's literature in the Discussion.

## Information elements (the checklist)

For each group of results discussed, work through:

1. Reference to the main **purpose or hypothesis** of the study, or summary of
   the main activity.
2. **Restatement of the most important findings**, in order of significance:
   i. whether they support the original hypothesis / answer the research
      question;
   ii. whether they agree with other researchers' findings.
3. **Explanations** for the findings, supported by literature; or reasoned
   speculations, also supported.
4. **Limitations** that restrict generalisation beyond the study conditions.
5. **Implications** — what the results mean in the broader field.
6. **Recommendations** for future research and/or practical applications.

Elements 2–5 often repeat for each result group.

## Negotiating claim strength

The verbs carry the claim's strength. In *that*-clauses you have two levers:
the **main verb** and the **verb in the *that* clause**.

| Strength | Main verb | *That*-clause verb | Example |
|----------|-----------|--------------------|---------|
| **Very strong** | demonstrate (present) | regulate (present) | "Our experimental results **demonstrate** that space- and propagule-limitation both **regulate** S. muticum recruitment." |
| **Strong** | indicate (present) | will be determined (future) | "These results **indicate** that S. muticum recruitment under natural field conditions **will be determined** by the interaction between disturbance and propagule input." |
| **Moderate** | appears (present) | has (present) | "…it **appears** that GmDmt1;1 **has** the capacity to function in vivo as either an uptake or an efflux mechanism…" |
| **Weak** | suggests (present) | may be stabilised (modal) | "The presence of an IRE motif **suggests** that GmDmt1;1 mRNA **may be stabilised** by the binding of IRPs…" |

Match the strength to the data. Over-claiming triggers rejection;
under-claiming undersells the work.

### Strength ladder (main verb)

```
Strong   demonstrate / show / establish / prove
         indicate / illustrate
         appear to
Weak     suggest / hypothesise / speculate
```

### Strength ladder (*that*-clause verb)

```
Strong   present tense ("regulates")
         future ("will be determined")
         modal of certainty ("must")
Weak     modal of possibility ("may", "could", "might")
```

An alternative without a *that*-clause uses a noun-phrase object:

> "Previous studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between
> propagule pressure and the establishment success of non-native species."

This avoids the tense decision in the second clause — useful when you want to
hedge.

## Diplomacy when disagreeing

Reformulate to present your **own** strong points rather than attacking
others'. The implication does the work.

| ✕ Direct criticism | ✓ Diplomatic reformulation |
|--------------------|----------------------------|
| "Smith's study was underpowered." | "Smith's study **may have been** underpowered." |
| — | "Our study had **sufficient statistical power** to detect …" (implicit contrast) |

For EAL writers: paraphrasing can shift emphasis. Have a co-author check that
your restatement of others' work preserves their intended meaning.
