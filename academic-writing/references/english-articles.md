# English Articles (a/an/the) and which vs that

> When to read: editing for article correctness; punctuating relative clauses.
> Source: Cargill & O'Connor (2009), Ch. 17.6–17.7.

These two topics cause the most errors that referees flag. The rules below are
compressed; the underlying principle is always **generic vs specific**.

## Generic noun phrases

A generic NP refers to *any or all* members of a class. Four ways to write
one:

1. **Countable noun, plural, no article.**
   > "Healthy crops can contribute substantial cadmium to human diets."
2. **Countable noun, singular, with *a/an*.**
   > "A healthy crop can contribute substantial cadmium to human diets."
3. **Uncountable noun, no article.** (Uncountable nouns never take plural.)
   > "Cadmium exists in soils in many forms."
   > "Manipulation of soil pH can be effective in managing Cd contamination."
4. **Singular countable noun + *the*** (often for living things or equipment;
   plural-without-article is usually also valid).
   > "The earthworm can be found in many types of soil." (= Earthworms can…)

**For science writing:** as long as you mean the noun as a concept/class, it
stays generic — even after the first mention. Unlearn the school rule "a noun
becomes specific after first use".

## Specific noun phrases

A specific NP refers to particular members the reader and writer can identify.
It uses **the**. Three reasons a NP becomes specific:

1. **Shared/assumed knowledge** of one referent.
   > "…has been accelerating in **the** world."
2. **Pointing back** to old information already introduced.
   > "A pot experiment was conducted in an acid soil. **The** experiment
   > showed…"
3. **Pointing forward** to information that specifies which one(s).
   > "The aim of this study was to investigate **the** effect of liming on Cd
   > uptake."

**Useful rule of thumb:** in the structure **NP1 + of + NP2**, the first NP is
specific about 85% of the time — use **the** unless you are sure the whole
phrase is generic.

## `which` vs `that` and commas

Relative clauses come in two kinds.

### Defining clause — essential to meaning

If you omit it, the sentence no longer makes sense. It **defines** which noun
the sentence is about.

> "Land **which is surrounded by water** is an island."
> (Without the clause: "Land is an island." Nonsense.)

- **No commas** around a defining clause.
- **UK/AU/NZ practice:** `which` or `that`. **US practice (and MS Word's
  grammar checker):** `that` only. Pick one convention and follow the journal's
  style.

### Non-defining clause — extra, non-essential information

Test: insert "by the way" after `which`. If it sounds right, the clause is
non-defining.

> "Tasmania, **which is surrounded by the waters of Bass Strait**, is an
> island of great natural beauty."
> (= Tasmania, which by the way is surrounded…, is…)

- **Commas** around it: two commas mid-sentence, one comma at the end.
- Can **only** begin with **`which`**, never `that`.

### Same rule when `which + verb` is omitted

> "Tasmania, **surrounded by the waters of Bass Strait**, is an island…"
> "Land **surrounded by water** is an island."

## Practice items (Task 17.9 style)

Punctuate (answers in the book):

1. Lime which raises the pH of the soil to a level more suitable for crops is
   injected into the soil using a pneumatic injector.
2. Manipulation which involves adding or deleting genetic information is
   referred to as genetic engineering.
3. Non-cereal phases which are essential for the improvement of soil fertility
   break disease cycles and replace important soil nutrients.
4. Senescence which is the aging of plant parts is caused by ethylene that the
   plant produces.
5. Opportunities that arise from the economically buoyant nature of domestic
   wine production must be identified and carefully assessed.
6. Lucerne is a drought-hardy perennial legume which produces high-quality
   forage.

(Items 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 contain non-defining clauses needing comma pairs; item 5
is a defining clause with `that` and no commas.)
