# Writing the Introduction

> When to read: drafting or revising the Introduction. Source: Cargill &
> O'Connor (2009), Ch. 8.

## The five stages

A compelling Introduction moves through five stages (after Weissberg & Buker
1990). Stages may repeat (especially 2/3) but all five should be detectable.

| Stage | Content | Typical tense |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| 1 | Broad setting — the field's importance, claimed centrality | Present / present perfect |
| 2 | Previous work on the specific problem — foundation of what is known | Past / present perfect |
| 3 | The gap or research niche — what is still unknown or contested | Present perfect ("has not been established") |
| 4 | The purpose / aim / main activity of this paper | Past ("We aimed to…") |
| 5 | *(Optional)* Positive value or justification for the study | — |

## Country → province → city (Stage 1)

Stage 1 narrows from a broad area ("country") through a sub-area ("province")
to the specific topic ("city"). The "city" is **not** the purpose of the study;
it is the specific topic area the paper addresses.

- **Country**: cybersecurity knowledge evaluation for large language models
- **Province**: benchmark-based evaluation of small language models
- **City**: evaluation of open-source SLMs (0.6B–31B) on the CyberMetric
  benchmark across multiple dataset scales

## Signal words for the gap (Stage 3)

Stage 3 sentences flag that a need exists. Common signal words:

- *however*, *although*, *despite*
- *remains a major challenge*
- *rarely*, *few studies*, *little information*
- *not well understood*, *unclear*, *presently unclear*
- *needs further investigation*

Example (Britton-Simmons & Abbott 2008):

> "Despite its acknowledged importance, propagule pressure has rarely been
> manipulated experimentally and the interaction of propagule pressure with
> other processes that regulate invasion success is not well understood."

## Citation styles (Stages 2–3)

Three styles, chosen to advance the argument:

**Information-prominent** (default in science):
> "…since the development of synthetic fibres (Smith 2000)."

**Author-prominent style 1** (you agree):
> "As Smith (2000) pointed out, the wool industry is experiencing
> difficulties…"

**Author-prominent style 2** (flag a contrast):
> "Smith (2000) argued that … However, Jones et al. (2004) found that …"

**Weak author-prominent** (topic sentence for a new sub-argument):
> "Several authors have reported that … (Smith 2000, Wilson 2003, Nguyen
> 2005)."

Beware over-using author-prominent style: it makes text read like a list, not
an argument. Use it sparingly, approaching the gap.

## Avoiding plagiarism

- Every idea or fact from another source must be cited **in the sentence where
  it appears** — not "somewhere nearby".
- Direct quotations ("…") are extremely rare in science writing; paraphrase.
- Reusing the **structure** of another author's sentence with your own content
  is acceptable (this is the "sentence template" technique — see
  `sentence-templates.md`). Reusing their content without citation is not.

Plagiarism test (McNeill et al. 1997 example):

| Version | Verdict |
|---------|---------|
| "Russell and Fillery (1996) … have shown that … but they indicated that the technique was not adaptable to all plants." | Cited correctly — the limitation is attributed. |
| "Russell and Fillery (1996) … have shown that …. However, this technique is not adaptable to all plants." | **Plagiarised** — the limitation now reads as the writer's own claim. |

## Suggested drafting order

1. **Stage 4** first (the aim) — easiest to write, anchors everything.
2. **Stage 3** (the gap) — leads into Stage 4.
3. **Stage 1** (the setting) — chosen to grab the target audience.
4. **Stage 2** (the literature foundation) — the most time-consuming.
5. Combine, add transitions, check that all title keywords appear in Stages
   3–4 so the title's promise is met.

## Editing for logical flow

Five strategies (apply when revising):

1. **Always introduce ideas** — informative subheadings; topic sentences that
   orient the reader and link to the previous paragraph.
2. **General → specific** within each paragraph. If a general sentence lands
   late, move it first.
3. **Old information before new information** in every sentence.
4. **Link within the first 7–9 words** of each sentence to something already
   established.
5. **Subject + verb within the first 7–9 words.** No top-heavy sentences.

Top-heavy fix example:

> ✕ "Actual evapotranspiration (T) for each crop, defined as the amount of
>   precipitation for the period between sowing and harvesting the particular
>   crop plus or minus the change in soil water storage in the 2m soil profile,
>   was computed by the soil water balance equation."
>
> ✓ "Actual evapotranspiration (T) for each crop was computed by the soil
>   water balance equation, as the amount of precipitation between sowing and
>   harvesting plus or minus the change in soil water storage in the 2m
>   profile."
