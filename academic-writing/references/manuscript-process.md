# Preparing a Manuscript — Process and Checklist

> When to read: end-to-end preparation, final editing, pre-review. Source:
> Cargill & O'Connor (2009), Ch. 15.

## Initial preparation (14 steps)

1. Select a "package" of results that makes a paper. Agree with co-authors:
   - Take-home messages (the story)
   - Is this the best data package? Add or remove to keep one coherent story?
   - Target audience and target journal
   - Who does what; author order; acknowledgements
   - Timeline and co-author review points
2. Give a short talk to colleagues: Stages 2–3 of the Intro, the aim, key
   methods, all data, the discussion. Collect feedback on clarity and
   questions.
3. Get the target journal's Instructions to Contributors; set up a document
   template.
4. Refine tables and figures (per the journal's guidelines). Check each is
   necessary; combine or delete. Make the take-home message of each visible.
5. Draft the **Results** section, highlighting the take-home messages.
6. Bullet-point ideas for the **Discussion**.
7. Draft or refine the **Title** to capture the main message.
8. Draft the **Methods**.
9. Draft the **Introduction**. Consider writing the stages in order **4 → 3 →
   1 → 2**, with Stage 5 where it best fits.
10. Draft the **Discussion** (and Conclusion, if separate).
11. Draft the **Abstract/Summary**.
12. Draft a set of **keywords**.
13. Assemble a complete first draft.
14. Refine using the editing procedure below.

## Editing procedure

1. **Put the draft aside** ≥ 48 hours. You need fresh eyes to read what you
   actually wrote.
2. Print a paper copy. Read through marking problems in the margin; **don't
   fix** anything yet.
3. Return to the start and fix each marked problem.
4. Repeat content-edit passes as many times as needed.
5. Only when the **science** is settled, edit for **discourse features**:
   - Subheadings where needed
   - Topic sentences on paragraphs
   - General → specific within paragraphs
   - Old information before new
6. Edit **spelling, punctuation, grammar**. Keep the spelling checker on but
   remember its limits (`there` vs `their`, `it's` vs `its` won't be flagged).
   Add technical terms to the dictionary carefully. For EAL writers: use a
   ruler on hard copy, reading the **last** sentence first and moving upward,
   to focus on grammar without distraction from content.
7. Edit **referencing**:
   - Every in-text citation has a list entry; every list entry is cited.
   - Style (punctuation, italics, capitalisation) matches the journal exactly.
8. Edit **layout** — headings stay with following text; running headers per
   the journal; tables/figures placed per Instructions.
9. **Final read-through** for "little" mistakes. Ask a colleague to do the
   same.

## Pre-review checklist (22 points)

Give this to a critical reader before submission, or use it yourself.

| # | Criterion |
|---|-----------|
| 1 | Does the title reflect the content accurately? |
| 2 | Are the significant words in the title near the beginning? |
| 3 | Does the Introduction begin with the big issue and narrow to the specific topic? |
| 4 | Does the Introduction locate the study in the recent international literature? |
| 5 | Does the Introduction highlight a gap the research fills? (Says why the work was done?) |
| 6 | Does the Introduction end with a clear aim/hypothesis or summary of main activity? |
| 7 | Are the methods, including statistics, appropriate for the questions addressed? |
| 8 | Are materials and methods detailed enough to convince a reader of credibility? |
| 9 | Do the results answer the questions raised in the Introduction? |
| 10 | Are results in a logical order (matching methods or discussion)? |
| 11 | Are all tables and figures needed? Could any be combined or deleted? |
| 12 | Do all tables and figures stand alone (understandable without the text)? |
| 13 | Does the Discussion begin with a reference to the original aim/hypothesis? |
| 14 | Are results compared with relevant literature? Are explanations/speculations included for similarities, differences, other outcomes? |
| 15 | Are appropriate statements made about wider significance, limitations, implications for practice and future research? |
| 16 | Does the paper end with an appropriate conclusion emphasising the key message(s) and significance? |
| 17 | Is the reference list complete (all listed works cited; all cited works listed)? |
| 18 | Are the reference list and in-text references formatted accurately and in the journal's style? |
| 19 | Does the Abstract include all elements the journal requires and highlight the key results and significance? |
| 20 | Does the Abstract adhere to the word limit and prescribed format? |
| 21 | Are the selected keywords those that best let the full range of prospective readers locate the article? |
| 22 | What additional comments would strengthen the paper? |

Consider asking an experienced colleague to **pre-review** — read it as if
refereeing for the journal. Hand them the Referee's Evaluation Form from
`academic-evaluation/references/referee-criteria.md`.
