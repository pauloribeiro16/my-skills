# Writing the Results section

> When to read: drafting Results, designing figures/tables. Source: Cargill &
> O'Connor (2009), Ch. 4–6.

## The results "story" comes first

Before writing any prose, identify the **story** the data tell and the
**take-home message** (what readers remember after putting the paper down).
Then refine figures and tables so each one carries one or two bullet points of
that story.

The four questions that focus drafting (answer in English, even if it is not
your first language):

1. What do my results **say**? (≤ 2 sentences, no background)
2. What do these results **mean** in context? (the conclusions)
3. **Who** needs to know? (the audience for this paper)
4. **Why** do they need to know? (the contribution; what others would miss)

## Figure, table, or text?

| Best for | Table | Figure |
|----------|-------|--------|
| Working with | number (exact values) | shape (pattern) |
| Concentrating on | individual data values | overall pattern |
| When precise values are | more important | less important |

- **Tables**: raw or processed data, calculated components, multiple
  comparisons in many directions.
- **Figures**: overall trend, "picture" comprehension, simple comparisons
  between few elements.
- **Text**: anything expressible in one or two sentences.

Never duplicate data across text and illustrations.

## Designing figures

Design around the point you want to land most strongly. Decide **before**
opening graphics software:

- Which variable gets the heaviest line/most prominent symbol?
- Are you emphasising differences or similarities?
- What scale, intervals, max/min, statistical representation are most meaningful?

Common figure types:

- **Pie charts** — proportions of a whole
- **Column/bar charts** — comparing independent categories
- **Line charts** — sequence in time/space, dependent relationships
- **Radar charts** — categories not directly comparable

Common figure weaknesses (referees flag these):

- Wrong figure type — relationships look real when they aren't (or vice versa)
- Weak descriptive title where a story-telling title would work
- Data repeated from text or tables
- Symbols/lines don't emphasise the main result
- Clutter (too many legend symbols, numbers, axis divisions)
- Axes labelled with jargon, not plain terms
- Exact numbers shown when only approximate values matter

Figures look best at **3:2 proportions**; box a sparse figure, leave a dense
one unboxed. Shrink to the journal's column width and check clarity survives.

### Before/after example (Cargill Fig. 5.1 → 5.2)

**Before** — descriptive title, error bars, legend box:
> "Comparisons of root surface phosphatase activity of wheat plants for
> Control (CK), exclusively chemical fertilizer (CF), combined application of
> chemical fertilizer and wheat straw (CS), and farmyard manure (CM)
> treatments. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean."

**After** — story-telling title, LSD bar, symbols described in legend:
> "Root surface phosphatase activity of wheat plants differed after soil
> amendment with different fertilizer treatments. Phosphatase activity was
> highest in farmyard manure (Δ) treatments followed by combined application
> of chemical fertilizer and wheat straw (■), chemical fertilizer alone (□),
> and control/no amendment (○) treatments. Phosphatase activity declined over
> 5 weeks for all treatments. Least significant difference (LSD; two-way
> ANOVA, P ≤ 0.05) is 0.39 mg/g/h."

The improved version (i) removes error bars and uses an LSD bar, (ii) keeps
the same open/closed symbol for the key comparison, (iii) describes symbols in
the legend not in an inserted box, (iv) retitles the *x* axis, and (v) changes
the title from descriptive to story-telling.

## Designing tables

Common table weaknesses:

- Weak descriptive title where a story-telling title would work
- Unnecessary or redundant data (not referred to in text, constant columns)
- Non-significant or over-precise numbers (false accuracy, clutter)
- Omitted data the reader needs for calculations
- Rows not sorted to highlight the main result

Sort rows to surface the story. Round means and SDs back to avoid false
accuracy. Use a small break between individual data and summary rows.

## Figure legends and table titles — five parts

1. A **title** that summarises what the figure/table is about (story-telling
   beats descriptive).
2. Details of results or models shown.
3. Additional explanation of components, methods, or essential context.
4. Description of units or statistical notation.
5. Explanation of any other symbols or notation.

Tables tend to have brief Parts 2–3 and no Part 5.

## Writing about results

Results sentences do three things:

- **Highlight** the important findings
- **Locate** the figure/table where they are found
- **Comment** (but do **not** discuss) the results

Combined highlight + location:
> "Measurements of root length density (Figure 3) revealed that the majority
> of roots of both cultivars were found in the upper substrate layers."

Separate location:
> "Figure 17 shows the average number of visits per bird."

Present results in the **same order** as the methods, using the same
subheadings.

## Verb tense in Results

| Tense | Use | Example |
|-------|-----|---------|
| Past (active or passive) | The completed study — what was done and found | "Antibodies were raised in rabbits…" / "The anti-GmDMT1 antiserum identified a 67-kDa protein…" |
| Present | Always-true situations; the document itself | "The effect of urea concentration … is summarised in Table 1." |
| Modal (*may*, *could*) | Comments, especially in *that*-clauses | "The increase in size may be related to extensive post-translational modification…" |

**Do not** repeat in prose every number from a table or figure. Highlight only
the most important findings — especially those the Discussion will pick up.
