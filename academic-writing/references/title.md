# Writing the Title

> When to read: drafting or revising the title. Source: Cargill & O'Connor
> (2009), Ch. 10.

The title is the only part many readers ever see. It must **attract the right
readers** and **indicate the content** clearly enough to be found in searches.

## Strategy 1 — Maximum relevant information, concisely

Journals ask for "a concise and informative title (as short as possible)". The
more revealing the title, the more easily readers can judge relevance.

## Strategy 2 — Keywords at the front

Place the most important keyword(s) at the **beginning** of the title. This
helps literature-scanning services and catches a browsing reader's eye.

| ✕ Keywords buried | ✓ Keywords front-loaded |
|-------------------|-------------------------|
| Effects of added calcium on salinity tolerance of tomato | Calcium addition improves salinity tolerance of tomato |

A colon (`:`) or dash (`–`) can separate a front-loaded keyword section from an
explanatory tail:

> ✓ Disturbance, invasion, and reinvasion: managing the weed-shaped hole in
>   disturbed ecosystems
> ✓ Methylamine/ammonium uptake systems in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*:
>   multiplicity and regulation
> ✓ Resistance to infection with intra-cellular parasites – identification
>   of a candidate gene

## Strategy 3 — Noun phrase, statement, or question?

| Form | When it works | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|
| **Noun phrase** (traditional) | Most cases — brief, informative, keywords up front | "Iron uptake by symbiosomes from soybean root nodules" |
| **Statement** (subject + verb) | A specific question with a clear answer; replaces vague "The effects of…" | "Calcium addition improves salinity tolerance of tomato" |
| **Question** | No simple answer; provokes curiosity | "Which insect introductions succeed and which fail?" |

Statements are only suitable for papers that address a specific question and
present a non-complex answer. They give more explicit information about the
result than a noun phrase does.

A statement fixes vague openers:
> ✕ "Evidence of involvement of proteinaceous toxins from *Pyrenophora teres*
>   in net blotch of barley"
> ✓ "Proteinaceous metabolites from *Pyrenophora teres* contribute to symptom
>   development of barley net blotch"

## Strategy 4 — Avoid ambiguity in noun phrases

Strings of nouns used as adjectives cause ambiguity. Limit noun-noun phrases
to **three words**, and only if no misunderstanding is possible.

| Ambiguous | Unambiguous |
|-----------|-------------|
| enzymatic activity suppression (suppression **of** or **by** enzymatic activity?) | suppression of enzymatic activity |
| soybean seedling growth suppression | suppression of soybean seedling growth |

When a noun modifies another noun, the modifier is always **singular**:
- food for dogs → dog food
- disturbance by herbivores → herbivore disturbance
- nodules on soybean roots → soybean root nodules

## Process tip

Keep a list of candidate titles as you draft. Choose the final one **at the
end** of the writing process, when the paper's key message is settled.
Re-reading the title against the finished Discussion often surfaces a better
formulation.

Check the target journal for any length limit (words or characters) and any
convention on subtitles.
